Tell us what your preferred programming language is and why?

C++. It forces me to think about memory, performance, concurrency, data structures, and what the machine underneath is actually doing. That level of control is what I find interesting about performance-sensitive work. In practice, most of my professional time has gone into Go and Dart. Go for backend and service work: simplicity, a concurrency model that doesn't fight you, good tooling, and services that stay reliable. Dart through Flutter and FlutterFlow, working on and debugging mobile applications. I don't treat a language as an identity. C++ is my preference because I like being close to the system; Go and Dart taught me to value simplicity, maintainability and the time it takes to build something. If a problem calls for a language I don't know yet, I'll learn it. The principles transfer.

We are also interested to know how and why you started using Linux professionally.

It started at university, for a reason that had nothing to do with employability. I wanted to know what was happening underneath the applications I was writing. Linux let me work directly with the operating system, the tools, networking, processes, permissions and resources, instead of treating all of it as a black box. The move from development environment to daily driver was gradual. Backend services, cloud systems, deployments, debugging, mobile development — Linux ended up underneath all of it. I've used Linux-based environments for development, testing and troubleshooting, and worked with AWS, GCP and Huawei Cloud environments. Support work at FlutterFlow pushed it further. A lot of the issues I investigated couldn't be solved at the application layer. I had to understand how the components interacted, reproduce the problem, read the logs, test configurations, and then explain it clearly enough for engineering to act on. Control and transparency drew me in first. I stayed because I'd rather understand a system end to end and fix the real problem than work around it. That's most of why Canonical interests me.

In your most recent role, what did you enjoy most, and what did you achieve that you consider exceptional?

The best part of FlutterFlow was sitting between the technology, the customer and the product. A customer arrives with a symptom, and the actual job is working out what they're really trying to do, turning that into something clear, and feeding whatever keeps recurring back to engineering and product so it gets fixed once rather than fifty times. One week stands out as the closest thing to exceptional. Our support team was four people, and on a single Friday we took more than 500 inbound tickets, immediately before a company offsite. I worked through the weekend, and we had the backlog under 30 tickets within about a week. We held a CSAT above 90% that week and around 92% for the month. I wouldn't want to repeat it, but I know what I'm capable of when the pressure is real. Separately, I supported a major pricing and plan migration where more than 150 customers needed manual upgrades in Stripe within two days, on top of the normal queue. There was also the less visible work: knowledge-base content, spotting recurring product problems, testing and debugging, and keeping engineering informed about what was actually breaking for customers. What I found most rewarding was being able to see the line from a support ticket to a product change to a customer having a better time.

And, in the end, walk us through some of the proudest moments in your career and some of the downfalls.

The proudest ones are mostly about building something that mattered to somebody. Co-founding DawaMom at university is the earliest. A maternal-health initiative built around helping frontline healthcare workers make better, data-informed decisions, and it taught me that technology only means something when it addresses a problem people actually have. Around the same time I was Vice President and later Acting President of the University of Zambia Computer Society, which was my first experience of leading, organising people and building a community around technology. More recently I won the DreamFlow no-code hackathon for best-designed application, out of more than 600 submissions. In 2021 I was awarded Best International Accomplishment for the work done at Dawa Health. I've also been named one of the top 30 under 30 most inspiring youth in Zambia by Dream Factory Zambia, and received the most innovative solution award from Genius Education Zambia. Two companies co-founded is probably the headline. Professionally it's FlutterFlow, during that high-volume period. Four of us, more than 500 tickets in one Friday, the backlog down to under 30 within about a week, CSAT above 90% for the week. What I took from it had nothing to do with the numbers. It's that I'll take ownership when a situation needs it, and help a team recover. Then StackOne and MediTracker. Running a company alongside a job forced me to learn product, engineering, customer discovery, operations and leadership at the same time. Talking to more than 100 pharmacies while building MediTracker taught me that the best product insight comes from listening rather than assuming. The downfalls are real too. Products and ideas I put serious time into that went nowhere. Periods where I spread myself across too many things at once and did none of them properly. Technical and product decisions I'd make differently now. What those taught me: validate earlier, prioritise harder, communicate sooner, and don't confuse being busy with creating value. I'm much more deliberate about understanding a problem before building, and about checking whether what I built is actually working. I don't really separate the setbacks from the successes. Both shaped how I work.
