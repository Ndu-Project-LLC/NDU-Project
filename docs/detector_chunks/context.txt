Outline your thoughts on the mission of Canonical. What is it about the company's purpose and goals that is most appealing to you?

The phrase that stays with me is the one about amplifying the impact of open source. My first contact with that idea was literal, and slightly stubborn: I dual-booted Ubuntu early on mostly to force myself to learn Linux properly. I've been in that ecosystem ever since. The scale of it, mostly. A tool that one company writes fixes that company's problem. A tool an entire ecosystem can use, extend and improve fixes it for everyone who comes after. Most of my career has been building products for specific users, one company at a time, and I'd rather the next stretch of it were work an entire ecosystem depends on. That's part of why I'm applying. Canonical isn't only publishing software either. It keeps the parts running that nobody notices until they stop: security updates, upgrade paths, documentation. Ubuntu is the obvious example. Canonical describes its own history as closely tied to the Ubuntu community and to the goal of making open source easier to adopt and operate, which is a fair summary of what I find interesting. One security patch can land on thousands of machines and nobody knows whose afternoon it was. That's the bit I keep coming back to.

Are there any elements of the company's goals that you are unsure about?

One, and it isn't a doubt about the mission so much as a tension I'd want to understand better: how Canonical weighs commercial customers against upstream communities against the long-term technical direction of its own projects. A customer wants a workaround by Friday. Upstream may need an architectural change and a release cycle. Canonical's own description of Sustaining Engineering admits as much, since the team is expected to provide workarounds while also driving fixes that can be released upstream and into Ubuntu. I don't think that's a problem to be solved. It's the job. But I'd want to know how engineers actually make those calls, and what Canonical measures to decide whether it got the balance right.

Do you see any risks in Canonical's mission?

Yes, a few. Complexity worries me most. The more open source sits inside critical infrastructure, the more dependencies, integrations and failure modes come with it, and holding the line on reliability and security gets harder every year. Speed is the other tension. Open source moves fast, but infrastructure software also has to stay compatible, upgrade predictably and take security fixes for years. Move too fast and you pay for it for a decade. And coordination is the hard one. Collaboration is the whole mechanism here, but projects, maintainers, vendors and users all want different things, and nobody can order them to agree. None of that argues against the mission. It's the reason Sustaining Engineering exists: people whose whole job is to absorb the mess, through deep debugging, upstream work, documentation, testing, and maintenance nobody notices.

Which other companies are building the sort of products you would like to work on?

Red Hat and SUSE, obviously. Both work on enterprise Linux and open-source infrastructure for a living. I follow the cloud-native crowd as well, the people behind Kubernetes, containerisation and distributed systems, because a lot of the interesting systems work is happening there now. Product similarity wouldn't decide it for me, though. Breadth is the draw: Ubuntu, Linux, infrastructure, global customers, and an engineering organisation that spans kernel, embedded Linux, Go, containers and virtualisation, security, and performance and correctness. Becoming a strong systems engineer matters more to me than specialising early in a single application layer.

This role in the Sustaining Engineering Group is critical for resolving complex issues and bugs for our customers and the wider community. Please explain your interest in this position compared to a role in a product development or engineering team.

Because I've already been doing a version of this work, and it was the part I liked most. At FlutterFlow that was the job: take a customer's broken thing, work out what was really happening, reproduce it, test the fixes I could think of, decide whether the fault was ours or in their environment, and hand engineering something actionable. Frequently nobody had defined the problem properly. Frequently the customer wanted an answer the same day. I got comfortable with that. Sustaining Engineering takes the same work several layers deeper: critical issues anywhere in the open-source stack, debugging at whatever level the problem turns out to live at, workarounds when a fix isn't ready, patches developed with software engineers, and conversations with upstream communities. So the comparison with a product team is easy for me. Product work mostly asks what to build next. This asks why something is broken, who is affected, and how to fix it without breaking something else on the way. I want the second question. There's also the matter of what I want to get better at. I don't want a career where I only implement requirements somebody else has already worked out. I like forming a hypothesis, reproducing a failure, reading logs until the picture makes sense, narrowing it down, and finding the smallest change that actually holds.

Why do you most want to work for Canonical?

Two reasons, and neither is prestige. The first is that Canonical sits exactly where I want my skills to go next: Linux, open source, systems engineering, cloud infrastructure, debugging, large-scale reliability. I have breadth — Dart and Flutter, Go, cloud platforms, databases, APIs, support, product, a couple of companies. What I don't have yet is depth at the systems level, and that's the gap I want to close. The second is the feedback loop. Understand a real failure, investigate it, talk to whoever is affected, ship a workaround or a fix, and where it makes sense push the improvement upstream so nobody meets it again. I can see the result of my work in that loop, which matters to me more than it probably should. I'm comfortable being dropped into unfamiliar systems and expected to find my way around. My best work has come from being handed a problem and trusted to own it rather than from executing a spec. Canonical's combination of Linux, open source, global scale and engineering depth is where I'd like to do that.
